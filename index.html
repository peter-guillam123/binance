Twenty minutes, $6.3bn

On Thursday evening an attacker emptied wallets at Binance, the world’s biggest crypto exchange. It is the largest theft in crypto’s history, and investigators blame North Korea. Here is what happened, and what it tells us about crypto, in plain English.

Based on Guardian reporting, the crypto-tracing firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis, and statements from Binance. Five minutes to read. Open the extra detail in each section if you want more. Prefer to watch? Four short videos.

New to crypto? Start with the basics.

The basics

Crypto in two minutes

Crypto is money recorded on a public ledger instead of in a bank. Most people hold it through exchanges such as Binance, which keep it for them. That makes exchanges a rich target.

A cryptocurrency is digital money that no government or bank issues. Bitcoin, launched in 2009, was the first. Thousands have followed, and before the hack they were worth about $2.7tn in total.

Every payment is written to a : a shared record, copied across thousands of computers, that anyone can read and nobody can change. That matters here in two ways. Investigators could watch Binance’s money leave in real time. But no one, Binance included, could stop the payments or reverse them.

Owning crypto means holding a , a secret code that lets you spend it. Lose the code and the money is gone for good. So most people leave their coins with an , which holds the keys for them. Binance is the biggest, with 335 million registered users and about $163bn of customers’ assets, according to its 2025 annual report.

Bitcoin is in a slump. It hit a record of about $126,000 in October 2025. It fell to $60,062 in February and was around $80,000 before the hack, then dropped to $76,368. US interest rates rose in September, which tends to hurt riskier assets such as crypto.

The rules are still being written. The US passed its first stablecoin law, the Genius Act, in July 2025, but a wider bill on crypto markets stalled in the Senate in September. Since July 2026 only licensed crypto firms may serve customers in the EU. The UK’s new regime starts in October 2027.

Can people in the UK use Binance? Binance has no FCA authorisation and stopped signing up new UK customers in October 2023.

The break-in

How they got in

Binance says the attack may have started with a single hacked laptop. From there the attacker reached the system that pays out withdrawals, and made it pay them.

Binance has given only an early account, and promises a full technical report. Step through what we know so far.

Go deeperThe Bybit break-in, and what we still don’t know+

The previous record theft, in February 2025, followed a similar path. Attackers took about $1.5bn from Bybit, an exchange based in Dubai. The FBI blamed TraderTraitor, the same North Korean unit now blamed for Binance.

  1. On 4 February a developer at Safe{Wallet}, the wallet software Bybit used, ran a booby-trapped software project on their Mac.

  2. The attackers used it to steal the developer’s login to Amazon’s cloud, which let them into Safe’s systems without passing extra security checks.

  3. Around 19 February they planted hidden code in Safe’s website that switched on only for Bybit.

  4. On 21 February Bybit staff approved what looked like a routine transfer. In fact they handed control of the wallet to the attackers. Bybit’s chief executive, Ben Zhou, said every person who approved it had been shown a doctored screen.

What we still don’t know

  • How the engineer’s laptop was hacked.
  • Why no check stopped billions leaving in 20 minutes.
  • The final total. Estimates range from $5.3bn (Glassnode) to $6.4bn (Chainalysis); Binance says about $6.3bn.
  • When withdrawals will reopen. Binance’s co-chief executive Yi He said it expected to resume them within 48 hours.

The chase

The race to freeze the money

About half of what was taken was in stablecoins, which the companies behind them can freeze, and most of those have been. The other half is in coins that no one can freeze.

such as Tether’s USDT and Circle’s USDC are run by companies. Each keeps a list of blocked addresses, and tokens at a blocked address can’t move. Within hours Tether had frozen $1.57bn and Circle more than $800m.

Bitcoin and have no company behind them, so there is no one to ask. The thief knows this. Within hours they were swapping stablecoins for ether, according to the crypto news site Decrypt. Every dollar swapped before a freeze lands is out of reach.

Tether and Circle figures from Reuters and CNN. Binance puts the total frozen at about $3bn; the third bar is the difference, and includes firms such as World Liberty Financial and the Arbitrum network’s security council. TRM Labs counted $2.7bn frozen at 00:30 UTC on Friday.

Freezing is not the same as getting the money back. Returning frozen funds usually takes court orders, says Ari Redbord of TRM Labs. And the coins that can’t be frozen are already on the move: bitcoin worth about $1.4bn has been split across 19 wallets.

Go deeperHow stolen crypto gets cleaned+

North Korea’s hackers follow a routine. Chainalysis says it typically takes about 45 days.

  1. Swap. Anything that can be frozen is traded for ether within hours, often on automated trading services that have no company in charge.

  2. Hop. Services such as THORChain move funds between blockchains, often turning ether into bitcoin. About 72% of the Bybit haul went this way.

  3. Mix. such as Tornado Cash pool coins from many users so the trail goes cold. The investigator ZachXBT says some Binance funds were mixed with money from September’s Bitget hack.

  4. Cash out. Brokers who trade large sums privately, many of them in China, and online markets such as Cambodia’s Huione turn the coins into ordinary money.

How much comes back? Usually very little. After the 2022 Ronin hack, investigators seized $30m of the $620m taken. Bybit said 3.8% of its losses had been frozen. Binance is unusual: because so much of the haul was in stablecoins, about half was caught in the first few hours.

The suspects

North Korea’s crypto thieves

Two crypto-tracing firms blame TraderTraitor, an elite North Korean hacking unit. North Korea is now the world’s most successful crypto thief, and the money is thought to help pay for its weapons.

TRM Labs says it has “high confidence” that carried out the attack. Jonathan Levin, the chief executive of Chainalysis, said it was “likely done by DPRK group TraderTraitor due to links to previous hack”. DPRK is North Korea’s formal name. No government has yet named a culprit, and North Korea has not commented.

TraderTraitor is part of the wider group known as Lazarus, which the US says is run by North Korea’s main spy agency, the Reconnaissance General Bureau. South Korean officials estimate that North Korea has about 8,400 hackers.

    How they get in

    They rarely break the code. They trick the people who run it.

    The dream job

    Fake recruiters approach staff with tempting offers. The $620m Ronin theft in 2022 began when a senior engineer opened a job offer that carried spyware.

    The coding test

    Software developers are sent a test project to run as part of a job interview. Hidden inside is malware that steals passwords and crypto.

    The fake employee

    North Koreans get hired as remote IT workers under false names. The US says they used the identities of more than 80 Americans to work at more than 100 US firms.

    The weak supplier

    Instead of attacking an exchange head on, they hack a firm it relies on. That is how they got into Bybit.

    They now use AI too. Anthropic and Google have both reported North Korean operatives using their AI chatbots, and the security firm CrowdStrike says they use live deepfakes in job interviews.

    What the money pays for

    UN sanctions cut North Korea off from most legal trade. Its legal exports were worth about $469m in 2025; the largest category, which includes wigs and false eyelashes, made up 43.9% of that. The Binance haul is more than 13 times as much.

    Countries reporting to UN sanctions monitors have estimated that cyber theft brings in about half of North Korea’s foreign currency and pays for 40% of its weapons programmes. In 2023 a White House official said about half of its missile programme was funded by cyber-attacks and crypto theft.

    The fallout

    What happens now

    Binance says no customer will lose money. The bigger damage may be to trust in exchanges, which keep huge sums in one place.

    Will Binance customers lose money?

    Binance says not. “Bottom line – your money is safe, and we’re covering every loss,” said Richard Teng, its co-chief executive. It will pay from its own funds and from , an emergency fund held in bitcoin. SAFU holds 15,000 bitcoin, worth about $1.15bn, so most of the cost would fall on the company itself. Deposits are working; withdrawals are suspended for now.

    What does it mean for crypto?

    Big exchanges store vast sums in one place, so one successful break-in can do enormous damage. Cristiano Ventricelli of Moody’s Ratings said the theft “illustrates the risk concentration inherent in large, centralized intermediaries”, and highlights the risks of .

    Bernardo Brites, chief executive of the payments firm Trace Finance, expects customers to leave smaller exchanges that could not cover a big loss. He also worries that defence is falling behind: “It’s becoming very hard to counter hacks as AI can find vulnerabilities faster than you can fix them.” Last week security chiefs warned of a “vulnerability tsunami” as AI tools find flaws in widely used software.

    What to watch

    • Whether withdrawals reopen within the 48 hours Binance has promised.
    • How much more of the money is frozen, and whether any of it is returned.
    • Binance’s full technical report, and an updated count of the assets it holds for customers.
    • Whether the US or another government formally blames North Korea.
